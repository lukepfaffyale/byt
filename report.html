BYT

by Symbolon. Beat your Sym. Benefit from your Sym. Be everywhere.

1Mission and links

Our mission: to help people earn passive income while building the platform and the connections that AGI will need from people.

The idea began with a plain complaint: firms earn from people's data, and when a server is breached the person gets a credit alert and nothing else. Today people have jobs. Tomorrow is uncertain. As AI learns the work, the one thing it cannot make is a living person's own taste, judgment and choice. BYT is a tap-only game that turns those into a Sym: a digital you that you own. Plain code tests the Sym against picks it has never seen. Then it can answer and work while its person rests, and its person is paid. The more a person uses their Sym, the more it can earn (section 7). We show that potential with play money.

Phase 1 is built and live. On scripted test accounts the Sym beat the crowd's guess. On test accounts played by hand it did not. A study with real players is the next test, and one paid invoice is the first money milestone (sections 7 and 8).

2Motivation

Who has the problem. As AI learns the work, two groups are stuck, on two sides of one trade.

  • People. Online adults spend about 33 and a half hours a week on online media. That is close to 29 percent of their waking lives (DataReportal, Digital 2026 Global Overview Report, October 2025, from GWI data). Every tap tells someone what that person likes. That signal is sold, and the person is paid nothing and owns nothing at the end.
  • Organizations that need to know what people think. A brand testing a product, a recruiter looking for a kind of thinker, a school choosing a course. Today they buy a survey panel. Prolific is a large research panel. Researchers there are advised to pay people 12 US dollars an hour, and the platform adds a fee of 42.8 percent for companies (Prolific pricing and researcher help pages, read 21 November 2026). It takes hours or days, it reaches the people who like doing surveys, and each new question starts from zero.

What it costs them. The person gives away the one thing still scarce when AI does most work: taste, judgment and feeling.

The idea. Let the person keep that signal in something they own: a Sym. A symbolon was a token broken in two, and each half proved the other. The game builds the Sym from taps, tests it against its person, and then lets it answer for them while its person rests, with pay each time. Could an AI just make these people up? It can guess the crowd. It cannot know when a living person breaks from the crowd unless that person shows it. That is what AGI will need from people: our thesis, not yet a result.

Six things BYT does differently

  1. The Sym's code. Every input writes one block, in four letters like the bases of DNA: T a tap, G something proved, A an agency act, C a creator act. People provide the blocks, and the game counts how each code differs from every other. That difference is the asset: if every Sym were the same, no one would pay to ask.
  2. Proved, not claimed. A Sym commits its guesses before its person sees the cards. Plain code compares them and gives the certificate.
  3. A person checks before pay. A Sym's person approves or corrects each job first. A correction teaches the Sym.
  4. Groups, never one person. An agency sees totals over at least 5 Syms. It cannot type a question: it taps one from a fixed list.
  5. Three seats, one login, one economy. Anyone can play, run an agency and run a creator studio, and each seat wins when the others do (section 7).
  6. Worth is not weighed by money. What a Sym is paid never depends on how many credits its person holds.

4The agents

Four agents do the work. Each has one job, one read-only tool, and a check that is not a model. They never call each other. The database carries the hand-off, so the design has no loops between agents.

The agents work as a loop, not a line. When the Twin guesses a pick wrong in a battle, code hands the misses to the Profiler, which rewrites the beliefs that failed, and the player sees the before and after lines. When a person corrects a job answer, that correction is the next thing the Profiler reads. The Sym's voice grows the same way: plain code picks how the Guide speaks from the person's own taps (plain while it is new, then in the thinking style its person leans to most), so a model never picks its own voice. Each AI answer shows its real cost on the job and talk screens.

Model, and why

All four agents use gpt-6-luna on the Responses API, with a Portkey key or an OpenAI key, under the same limits and checks. It is cheap enough to run every time a player finishes a gate (prices are in section 5). Each run is capped at 4 model requests and 3 tool calls. A daily token budget stops spending, and a practice Sym that uses no AI takes over when there is no key or no budget.

What a model never decides

Certificates, pay, prices, what an agency sees, the Sym's letters, its code and the crew's lines are plain code. When an agency asks 194 Syms a question, no model runs at all: each Sym answers from its person's counted picks. That keeps the answers free and repeatable. A Sym with nothing counted on a question, or a tie, says "I do not know yet" and is left out of the totals. Tonight it is still paid, because the price is set before the question is known. Next: it is not paid, and it asks its person.

What was done to cut cost

  • The Twin reads beliefs, not raw picks. Its input stays near 2,700 tokens however long the player has played.
  • Prompt caching helped little: 15 percent of the Profiler's input, 7 percent of the Twin's.

What this shows.

  • On the scripted accounts, every method that knew the account beat the crowd's guess (35, 30 and 35 of 40, against 18). A steady pattern can be learned.
  • On the two accounts played by hand, no method did (11, 6 and 10 of 20, against 13). Twenty picks is too few to conclude from, and it is the result that matters most.
  • Plain counting matched or beat the AI, for nothing. Part of that is the test: the counter sees the trait tags and the AI does not, and scripted accounts pick by those tags.
  • The cut may hurt, and it saved no money here. A Twin run on beliefs cost 26 percent less and got 36 right against 45, which could be chance. With the Profiler counted, beliefs cost 0.0107 dollars and raw picks 0.0044, so beliefs pay off only when many runs reuse one set.
  • Beliefs still earn their place. A person can read, correct and delete them, and they are all the Worker is given about its person.

The crowd here is nearly all BOT Syms with random tastes, so its guess sits close to chance. Next, the Twin is given the counted picks for each option beside its beliefs, so it starts where counting stops and changes a guess only for a stated reason. Each battle will then score the Twin, the counter and the crowd on the same cards.

6Safety

Trust is the product, and the safest message is one nobody can send. In BYT no one can type to anyone else, and almost no one can type to a model: questions, jobs and cards are tapped from fixed lists. Apart from a nickname and a seat's name, the one place a player types (a line to their own Sym) is guarded before and after the model, is not stored, and reaches no other player. If the player taps yes, the Sym keeps one short fact in its own checked words. Kept facts reach the Profiler, the Twin and (when a line asks what the Sym knows) the Guide, with no name, after the prompt guard.

Real players. No study has run yet. The study, with its success line written first: at least 10 real adults, over 2 days, finish both battles (200 or more hidden picks), and everyone who signs up is reported. Success: the AI Twin beats the crowd's guess for at least 8 of 10 players and is 10 points ahead overall. The crowd counts real people only, and the result is reported either way. Also: time to the first certificate, who comes back, and one real asker with one real question, timed and priced against a panel.

What the game costs against how it is done today

Take one short question to 194 people. On Prolific that costs about 55.40 US dollars: 0.20 dollars for a one-minute answer at the advised pay, plus the 42.8 percent company fee. The next question costs the same again. In BYT the same question to 194 Syms returns at once and uses no AI tokens. If a credit were a cent and all 194 were active members, it would cost about 2.13 dollars: about 1 cent an answer, against about 29. It is not the same product: a Sym's answer is counted from its person's past taps, and how well it matches the person is not yet measured. Today 193 of the 194 are BOT Syms, so almost all of a first ask goes to the game's BOT wallet, not to a person.

Is it worth it to the people using it?

After a gate, a job or a question, the Sym or the crew asks one thing: was that worth it. One tap answers, and it is stored with no name. The About page shows the counts.

The first minute. A new player first sees an opening scene: 5 short lines in about 26 seconds say why the game exists and what a Sym is. A "How it works" pop-out gives four steps. Calibration is built to take about a minute: 8 taste picks, 3 one-move boards and 3 reaction rings. Each gate ends on "+N blocks", the Sym asks "May I earn?", and a line on the journey shows taps, blocks, asks and credits back. Two first outside testers, a friend and a family member of the author, tried the live game on 22 November 2026. The first found that the idea needed an introduction, so the opening scene and "How it works" were built. The second played after that and still got lost in the words between sign-up and the first cards: "I feel like an alien who doesn't know the language. What is a Sym, my own robot?" She found the games easy and the wording hard. In answer the first minute was cut to fewer words, "A Sym is a digital you" is now said at sign-up, and a new player sees one card and one button. Two testers are feedback, not a study, and they are not counted as evidence that a Sym knows its person. One person's feedback is not study evidence, so both boxes here stay open.

How the game would make money (proposed prices, all estimates)

With real money, card fees, payouts, identity checks and running costs would take about 17 credits of each 100 that reach people (Stripe's pricing pages, read 22 November 2026), so today's 10 percent would lose money. We propose a fee on top of what an agency spends on people: 20 percent to ask any Sym, 25 for members, 30 for Syms with a certificate. A paid panel adds 42.8. An agency may also pay for a connector that plugs BYT into its own tools, under the same rules. Players never pay to play or to earn. The strategy: quantity first. We call this the Cournot model, where rivals compete on how much they supply. The game wins by having the most proved, consenting Syms soonest: more people, more proved Syms, more agencies, more pay, more people. That supply is worth buying and hard to copy, because code gives each certificate and each Sym's data is its person's own. The race must never lower the bar for a certificate: the certificate is the product. The first customer we will ask is a campus recruiter. Recruiters can keep their job sites. BYT sells what those cannot: skills proved by code, answers before a meeting, pay only for checked answers, and meetings with consent.

The path. Our target is a rule of 70: revenue growth in percent plus profit margin in percent of 70 or more. In the base estimate, 3 paying agencies grow to 180 in three years. The rule is met each year by growth alone: operating margin stays below zero for all three years, and revenue ends near 250,000 dollars a year, which is small. If agencies spend half as much, the rule is not met.

No one is promised a return. Players put in no money, so no money of theirs is at risk. A creator's risk is a small, stated stake: 27 credits a step, given back if no one passes it in 90 days (proposed). An agency pays for a service.

8Limits and next steps

What Phase 1 has not met yet is real people, and most limits below come from that.

What the game measures, and what it does not. BYT measures which option a person taps on our own cards, how fast, and, on puzzles, how sure they say they are. From those taps a Sym guesses later taps, and code checks each guess. The four letters, the thinking styles and the areas are a mirror made by counting picks. They are not a personality test, a brain measure or a health measure, and they can change as a person plays. A reaction time includes the phone's own delay. A card catches a small, everyday choice, and people choose differently in different places. So a Sym is a record of choices in a game. We call it proved only where it beats a fair guess. Tonight this record is a small working example, not a data set. To help future AI it needs many real people, visits on more than one day, and a separate, clear yes for that use.

What does not work yet

  • No study with real players yet. Every accuracy number is from test accounts, most of them scripted.
  • The crowd is mostly BOT Syms with random tastes. They are not evidence about people.
  • A group of 5 can still hold one real person. The minimum counts BOT Syms. A rule for 5 real people is written and tested but switched off: with one real player it would stop that player earning.
  • No way back after logging out. There is no password or recovery key, by choice, so that no email is stored.
  • Certificates cannot be checked by an outside party.
  • Phone, yes. Screen reader and keyboard, not yet. Every screen fits a phone with no scrolling. The checks were the author on an iPhone and AI test passes.
  • Job checks lean toward yes. A check pays more for "yes" than for a correction. The next version pays the same for both and adds blind checks.
